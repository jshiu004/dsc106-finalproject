What the average leaves out

Warming is global. Its pattern is regional and seasonal.

In this model and scenario, the global mean hides large differences between regions and between seasons. Understanding that pattern starts with asking both where and when.

The global map uses an Equal Earth projection to preserve relative geographic areas. Its original climate grid is 1.25° in latitude by 1.875° in longitude; the US view retains a rectangular projection. Twenty-year periods reduce the influence of individual unusually warm or cool years. Seasonal results pool the indicated months within each period; December–February is a climatological comparison, rather than a series of complete cross-year winters. A temperature difference of one kelvin equals a difference of one degree Celsius.

These findings describe one model realization under one scenario. They do not provide a range of model uncertainty, predict a specific future year, or describe every possible emissions pathway.

Progress and design challenges

What have we done so far?

We have selected ACCESS-CM2 monthly near surface air temperature data for the historical simulation and SSP2-4.5 scenario. We have calculated projected warming between our 1995–2014 baseline and 2081–2100 comparison period using month length weighting. Our notebook contains static global and US area maps, latitude comparisons, regional time trends, and a seasonal heatmap. We have exported annual and seasonal warming for every model grid cell, along with area weighted latitude band and global summaries, and checked them against the notebook results. We have built a guided scrolling story with linked global and latitude views, seasonal controls, a US area map, and a supporting data table, while time period exploration remains planned.

What will be the most challenging part to design, and why?

The most challenging part will be linking global, latitude band, and US views so readers retain their geographic context as they explore. A selected region must remain recognizable across maps and charts, even though each view summarizes the data differently. Seasonal comparisons need a consistent color scale so a change in color reflects a change in warming rather than a rescaled legend. Time period controls will also need clear labels that distinguish long term averages from individual years and preserve the historical baseline. We will need to balance those interactions with concise annotations so readers discover what a global average hides without losing the main story.
